# The lessons

The course starts before the board. A learner first needs to distinguish a symbol
from a quantity, a cell address from its contents, and selecting from submitting.
Chess then uses the same pictures, references and actions.

The curriculum has 17 families, labelled T0–T16, and **146 required distinctions**.
These are coverage obligations, not a count of screens. One lesson may establish
several related distinctions; several examples may be needed for one distinction.

Pieces use 5×5 patterns. The other side uses the same pattern with black and
white exchanged. Worked examples establish which piece each pattern represents.

## How a lesson teaches

A worked example shows an input and its result. A contrast changes something that
matters: a blocker, the moving side, a right retained from earlier play. Practice
asks the learner to predict or complete a result, with explicit carried feedback.
The passive route shows the full explanation without making a correct response
the price of admission.

The pieces use unfamiliar glyphs. Their identities emerge from setup, movement
and repeated relationships. Names such as “bishop” and “knight” in this guide are
our labels; the artifact does not depend on those words or familiar piece icons.

## Distinctions the course cannot blur

**Control and legal movement.** A pinned piece can still control a square.
A friendly piece can be defended without being capturable. A king capture must
be tested after the captured piece has gone and any line behind it has opened.

**A board and its history.** Identical occupancy can come with different turns,
castling rights, en-passant opportunities or repetition counts. Histories are
carried from legal play, with explicit shared segments and complete boundary
snapshots. The player follows those records; it does not reconstruct history
from the picture.

**The special moves.** Castling includes both pieces and tests the king's origin,
transit and destination. Returning a king or rook does not restore a lost right.
En passant expires immediately and removes a pawn off the destination square.
Promotion requires an explicit choice among queen, rook, bishop and knight.

**An opportunity and an ending.** A current threefold position or 100 halfmoves
without a pawn move or capture makes the corresponding claim available. The
claim is an explicit event. Checkmate, stalemate, agreement, resignation and a
recorded score have separate meanings.

## Exact facts and useful guidance

Tactical and pawn lessons establish observable relations: attacks, captures,
defenders, pins, forks, pawn structure and files. Strategic guidance uses those
facts but retains its limits. A material count or development principle does not
prove the best move.

Mating instruction includes king-and-queen and king-and-rook methods, avoiding
stalemate, and a finite assessed mating family. A played line demonstrates that
the line works. A forced claim needs the relevant replies accounted for; a
cooperative path alone does not prove it.

The move-check routine brings the subjects together: check the position and its
history, the move's legality, the resulting king safety and tactical consequences,
then apply limited strategic guidance. There is no engine ranking behind it.

## The chess boundary

Golden Board teaches the project's practical orthodox-chess profile. It includes
ordinary play, special moves, checkmate/stalemate, resignation, draw agreement,
current-position claims, and the three common dead classes: king versus king,
king and bishop versus king, and king and knight versus king.

Clocks, touch-move, arbiter penalties, intended-move claim procedure, automatic
fivefold/75-move adjudication, variants and a general dead-position solver are
outside this profile. An unsupported case is not silently given an exact verdict.
